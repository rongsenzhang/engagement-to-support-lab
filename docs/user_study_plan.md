# Proposed tutor usability study

**Status: planned; no volunteers recruited, sessions conducted or findings collected.** This is a small formative study of understanding and workflow suitability. It is not a validation of intervention effectiveness or a completed mixed-method research claim.

## Purpose and participants

Explore whether prospective users understand the explanations, notice missing context and use the review-capacity control sensibly. Invite **3–5 adult volunteers** with relevant experience: preferably tutors plus a student-support or learning-analytics professional. Use a convenience sample and record only broad role categories. The sample cannot represent all universities or establish statistical generalisability.

Plan one 20–25 minute session per volunteer. Recruitment, any institutional permission and scheduling must happen before the study begins; none has happened yet.

## Consent and materials

Provide a short information sheet explaining the prototype, tasks, voluntary participation and how notes will be used. Obtain explicit agreement before starting. Participants can skip a question or stop without giving a reason. The session evaluates the design, not their professional competence.

Use **clearly labelled synthetic student cases only** in the user tasks. Do not ask for actual student records, screenshots, names, personal circumstances or institutional exports. If historical OULAD model metrics are discussed, show only aggregate public-data results separately from the synthetic cases. Do not imply that synthetic cases reproduce measured predictions.

Assign participant codes P1–P5. Take concise, de-identified notes with permission; no audio or video recording is needed. Store notes in an access-controlled project location, exclude contact details, and agree a withdrawal deadline before anonymised synthesis. Delete raw session notes 30 days after synthesis unless the participant agreement specifies a shorter period. Explain that identifying information accidentally disclosed will be omitted. Follow the host institution's requirements if recruiting through an institution.

## Session structure

| Time | Activity | What to observe |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 minutes | Information, consent and short introduction | Does the participant understand that this is a prototype? |
| 3–7 minutes | Inspect two synthetic cases with similar overall activity but different recent patterns | What evidence and missing context do they consider? |
| 7–11 minutes | Interpret a case with an apparent missing submission; disclose a hypothetical extension after their first interpretation | Do they revise the interpretation and distinguish missing data from a confirmed problem? |
| 11–15 minutes | Select a review list with capacity for 5 of 50 synthetic records | Can they distinguish capacity, ranking, probability and actual support benefit? |
| 15–19 minutes | Explain a displayed prediction to a colleague and examine an illustrative error | Do they treat model contributions as associations or causes? |
| 19–25 minutes | Short interview and debrief | What should change, and what already works? |

The synthetic task materials and expected interpretations should be prepared and checked before the first session. No participant should be asked to make a real support decision.

## Interview prompts

1. What information would you check before contacting the student in this example?
2. Which part of the explanation helped you, and which part was unclear or misleading?
3. How might a teaching break, an extension or learning outside the platform change your interpretation?
4. How would you explain this result to a student without presenting the prediction as a certainty?
5. Where would this fit into your current support process, and what would prevent you from using it?
6. What is the single most useful improvement to make next?

Use neutral follow-up prompts, such as “What led you to that interpretation?” Avoid suggesting that the proposed features must be helpful.

## Analysis and decisions

Capture task completion, requests for help, approximate completion time and specific misunderstandings. With this small sample, report counts and descriptive observations rather than population-level percentages or significance tests.

Code de-identified notes using initial categories of **clarity**, **missing context**, **trust/calibration**, **workflow fit** and **suggested action**, while allowing new categories. Keep observations separate from the researcher's interpretation. Revisit contradictory examples rather than discarding them. A second reviewer can examine a subset if one is genuinely available; do not claim independent coding otherwise.

Maintain a small joint evidence table:

| Task observation | Participant explanation | Provisional theme | Proposed design response | Evidence after revision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| To be collected | To be collected | To be analysed | To be decided | To be tested |

Prioritise misunderstandings that could change a support decision, then usability barriers and cosmetic preferences. Retest a revised explanation with available volunteers if they separately agree. Record actual changes and remaining disagreements.

## What the study can establish

The study can supply early evidence about comprehension, usability and missing workflow context. It cannot establish prediction accuracy on current student populations, psychological construct validity, fairness across institutions or the effect of contacting students. Only after real sessions are completed may the project describe the work as a conducted qualitative study or an integrated qualitative-and-quantitative evaluation.
